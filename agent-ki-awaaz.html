Eight answers you can submit

Choice and fair pay
Q16
Distribution Commission Framework (Section 8)
Has open architecture served its purpose or is it simply increasing costs without adding any significant value for the end customer? Is there more that can be done in this area than proposed in the consultation paper.

Open architecture adds value only when the customer actually gets a choice at the point of advice. For most families, that point is their agent. I suggest allowing individual agents to represent up to three insurers in each segment (life, health and general), with the same training, suitability and record-keeping duties that apply today. When an agent can compare more than one insurer, they can match the product, price and claim service to the client instead of selling the only option they hold. This increases competition between insurers on value rather than on commission, and keeps accountability with the agent because every policy is tagged to the person who sold it. This matters most in smaller towns, where the local agent is often the only adviser a family has. Choice there should not depend on reaching a large broker or a website.

Q19
Distribution Commission Framework (Section 8)
Do the proposed commission limits arrive at a fair balance between policyholders' expectations of affordability and distributors' expectations of adequate remuneration?

The limits should reward the work that protects policyholders after the sale, not only the first sale. 1. Health renewals: an agent who keeps a family insured year after year, helps with claims and stops policies from lapsing adds real value. A slightly higher renewal limit for health policies that stay with the same agent would reward persistency and service, and discourage churning clients between policies. 2. Small-business group health: SME group policies need constant servicing, including adding and removing members, endorsements and claim support. Small employers have no HR team for this. The framework should recognise servicing of these policies, so that small businesses keep getting help and are not left unserved because the economics do not work. Both measures are paid for by better persistency and fewer lapses, which also lowers costs for insurers and policyholders.

Q15
Expense of Management Framework (Section 7)
Does the sector need a longer timeframe to achieve the proposed norms?

Yes, for distributors. Whatever final limits are notified, there should be a transition period of at least 12 months from the date the final regulations are published before the new commission structure applies to distributors. Agents and small intermediaries need time to adjust their businesses, understand new product sheets and suitability requirements, and complete any training needed. Insurers need time to update systems, contracts and reconciliation processes so that commission paid and commission received match. A clear, predictable timeline avoids disruption to policyholder service, especially renewals and claims support, during the change. It also matches the stated intent of an orderly transition to a better system.

Mis-selling and fair process
Q20
Distribution Commission Framework (Section 8)
Within the proposed commission limits, which are the possible measures to incentivize good market conduct? Vice-versa, what measures can be built in to disincentivize bad market conduct?

Good conduct: create a public clean-record badge for agents with no upheld complaints, low lapse rates and complete suitability records over, say, three years. Customers could see it on Bima Sugam and insurer websites. This costs nothing in commission and gives customers a simple way to choose a trustworthy adviser. Bad conduct: clawback on mis-sold or cancelled policies is fair, but it must follow due process. Before any mis-selling finding is recorded against an agent or made public, the agent should be told the complaint, see the evidence and have a right of reply within a fixed time. Many complaints arise from claim rejections or product terms the agent did not control. A short hearing step protects honest agents while still acting firmly against those who mis-sell.

Q25
Creating awareness against Mis-selling (Section 11)
Are the illustrations of mis-selling appropriate? Please suggest specific measures to disincentivize this.

The illustrations are useful. To disincentivise mis-selling effectively, measures should be targeted by channel, based on complaint and lapse data, rather than applied equally to every distributor. Publish complaint ratios, early lapse rates and free-look cancellation rates by channel and by distributor. Where a channel shows higher mis-selling, such as insurance bundled with loans or sold at the point of another transaction, apply stronger checks there: recorded consent, a cooling-off call from the insurer and a clear statement that the purchase is optional. Individual agents who work with their clients over many years should not carry the compliance burden designed for high-volume channels. A data-led approach puts effort where the problem actually is, and protects customers faster.

Q28
Market Infrastructure Institutions for Insurance (Section 13)
Do you agree with the role of MII as a systemic intervention for recalibrating economics of insurance distribution? Please suggest the nature of services you would like to be available on this platform.

Yes. One service that would help every policyholder and every distributor is a free, standard suitability form on Bima Sugam. The proposed suitability check before every sale is a good step. If each insurer and distributor builds its own form, costs rise and quality varies. A single, simple form on Bima Sugam, free for agents and customers to use, would give every sale the same documented needs analysis, store it with the policy record, and make it easy to review if a complaint arises later. Other useful services: a single view of all policies held by a family, renewal reminders, claim status tracking, and the agent's registration details and clean-record status, so customers can verify who they are dealing with.

Hospital costs: the real reason premiums rise
Q13
Expense of Management Framework (Section 7)
EoM has a direct relationship with affordability. A robust EoM framework is necessary for policyholder protection and orderly growth of the sector. What additional safeguards are necessary to address affordability?

In health insurance, affordability is decided mainly by hospital costs, not by distribution costs. Most of every health premium is paid out to hospitals as claims, and medical inflation is estimated at 12 to 14% a year. Cutting distributor pay alone will not stop premiums rising. I suggest these safeguards: 1. Benchmark package rates for common procedures, agreed between insurers and hospitals and published on the National Health Claims Exchange. 2. A clear ban on dual pricing: an insured patient must not be charged more than a cash patient for the same treatment. 3. A written cost estimate before every planned admission, and an itemised bill shared with the patient during the stay. 4. Publication of each insurer's renewal premium increases, with the reason given to policyholders, so that increases are linked to actual claims experience. 5. A report showing that savings from lower expenses of management reach policyholders as lower premiums. Without control of hospital pricing, savings in distribution will be absorbed by rising hospital bills, and policyholders will see no benefit.

Q32
Any other suggestion that you may have for these reforms
Are there any other aspects that need to be considered for distribution reforms and their effective implementation?

Distribution reform must go together with reform of hospital pricing. Otherwise families will keep paying more every year, whatever the commission rate. 1. Hospital pricing. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Health (176th Report, August 2026) recommended standard rates for common treatments, room charges benchmarked to local hotel tariffs, a binding cost estimate before complex treatment, a cap on the gap between purchase price and MRP of medicines and devices, and a ban on dual pricing for insured patients. The Clinical Establishments Rules already provide for a range of rates, but most States have not notified them. IRDAI should work with the Health Ministry and States so these measures are actually implemented, starting with benchmark package rates for insured patients. 2. Co-payment. Industry reports say a mandatory 10% co-payment on retail health claims is being considered. A mandatory co-pay moves the cost of hospital over-billing onto policyholders, including senior citizens and families facing large bills. Co-payment should stay optional, with a clear and published premium discount, and should not be made compulsory before benchmark rates and dual-pricing protection are in force. 3. Claim deductions. Policyholders are often surprised by deductions for consumables, room rent and non-payable items. Insurers should publish claim deduction data by hospital and by insurer on the Public Insurance Registry, and give a written reason for every deduction. 4. The agent's role in claims. When a claim is disputed, the family usually calls its agent first. Agents help with pre-authorisation, documents, deductions and reimbursement. The commission framework should recognise this servicing work, especially for health renewals and small-business group health, so that this support does not disappear just when hospital disputes are increasing. 5. Grievance redress. A simple, time-bound route to challenge an inflated hospital bill should be available to every patient, insured or not, with independent bill audit for high-value claims. These steps would make insurance more affordable, reduce disputes between insurers and hospitals, and build trust in the whole system.
